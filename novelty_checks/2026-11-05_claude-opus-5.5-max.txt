Why do skip connections improve neural networks? Give me all the reasons.

Provide a long elaborate answer. No online searching.

Skip connections change each layer’s job from “compute a new representation” to “add a correction to the one you were handed.” That one change makes deep networks much easier to optimize. Signals and gradients cross many layers without being repeatedly multiplied by weight matrices, the loss surface gets smoother, and layers that aren’t needed can quietly default to doing nothing. On top of that come real benefits for information flow, generalization and engineering. Here’s the full list, grouped by family.

Some reasons apply only to certain kinds of skip connection, so for reference:

Residual (additive): y = x + F(x). Used in ResNets and transformers.
Gated (highway): y = T(x)·H(x) + (1 − T(x))·x. Used in Highway networks; LSTMs and GRUs do this through time.
Dense (concatenative): y = [x, F(x)]. Used in DenseNet.
Long-range: links from encoder to decoder across resolutions. Used in U-Net, FCN and FPN.
Part 1: Optimization (they make deep nets trainable)
1. Extra depth stops hurting
The original ResNet paper (He et al., 2015) wasn’t mainly motivated by vanishing gradients. It was motivated by a paradox: adding layers to a plain network made its training error go up. A 56-layer plain net trained worse than a 20-layer one on CIFAR-10, and plain-34 trained worse than plain-18 on ImageNet. That can’t be overfitting, because it’s training error. The nets also used batch norm, and their gradient norms looked healthy.

It’s a paradox because a deeper net can always copy a shallower one and set its extra layers to the identity. So a solution at least as good exists, and the optimizer just doesn’t find it. A conv–BN–ReLU stack can express the identity (x = ReLU(x) − ReLU(−x)), but only with finely coordinated weights, and nothing steers training toward them.

With y = x + F(x), the identity is simply F = 0, which is where small initializations and weight decay already push. Unneeded blocks fade toward doing nothing, and needed ones learn something. Added depth becomes roughly “do no harm,” and the results flipped:

ResNet-34 beat ResNet-18 by about three points of top-1 error.
152-layer ResNets won ImageNet 2015.
A 1,202-layer ResNet trained to near-zero training error.
A neat corollary: weight decay pulls a plain layer toward the zero map, which destroys the signal. It pulls a residual branch toward the identity, which is harmless.

2. A gradient highway
Unroll the stack from block l up to block L:

x_L = x_l + Σ F(x_i)      (i = l … L−1)
∂E/∂x_l = ∂E/∂x_L · (I + ∂/∂x_l Σ F(x_i))
The I term delivers the error at the top directly to every lower block, untouched by any weight matrix (He et al., 2016). For the gradient to vanish, the second term would have to equal −I for every example in a batch, which essentially never happens. So gradients survive even when weights are tiny.

In a plain net, ∂E/∂x_l = ∂E/∂x_L · J_{L−1} ⋯ J_l, a product of many Jacobians. If their typical gain is a bit below 1, the product shrinks exponentially; a bit above 1, it explodes. In backprop terms, an addition node copies its incoming gradient unchanged to both inputs, so every block gets a direct line to the loss.

The shortcut has to stay clean. He et al. (2016) tried scaling the shortcut by 0.5, gating it, swapping it for a 1×1 conv, and applying dropout to it. Every variant did worse, some dramatically, because any factor λ on the shortcut compounds to roughly λ^depth. The same logic explains three things:

Moving BN and ReLU off the shortcut (“pre-activation” ResNets) let 1,001-layer nets train well.
Pre-LN transformers train more easily than Post-LN ones.
Ungated ResNets scaled further than highway networks, whose gates can close the highway.
3. Well-conditioned Jacobians
Gradient size isn’t enough. Ideally every direction propagates equally, meaning the input–output Jacobian’s singular values cluster near 1. This is called dynamical isometry (Saxe et al., 2014; Pennington et al., 2017). Plain nets reach it only with orthogonal initialization, suitable activations and careful tuning.

A residual block’s Jacobian is I + ∂F/∂x. With small branches it’s close to the identity, so the singular values cluster near 1 almost automatically. Tarnowski et al. (2019) showed ResNets reach dynamical isometry for essentially any activation function.

Mean-field theory says the same thing differently. In plain nets, two quantities evolve exponentially with depth: how similar two inputs’ representations are, and how large the gradients are. Inputs either collapse toward one representation or scramble into chaos, and only a thin “edge of chaos” is trainable at depth (Schoenholz et al., 2017). Skips make these dynamics subexponential, often polynomial (Yang & Schoenholz, 2017). The network then preserves the geometry of its inputs without knife-edge tuning.

4. Gradients that stay informative
Balduzzi et al. (2017) found a subtler problem in deep plain nets. Viewed as a function of the input, the gradient starts to look like white noise: gradients at nearby inputs become uncorrelated, with correlation decaying exponentially in depth. Such a gradient can have a perfectly healthy norm yet carry almost no usable information, so descent behaves more like a random walk. With skips, the correlation decays only sublinearly, so gradients stay coherent.

This plausibly resolves the original puzzle of plain nets that had normal-looking gradient norms but still trained badly. The norms were fine; the information content wasn’t.

5. A smoother loss landscape
Li et al. (2018) visualized loss surfaces and found that ResNet-56 sits in a smooth, nearly convex basin. Remove its skips and the same network’s landscape turns chaotic, full of sharp ravines and local minima. It gets worse with depth: the 110-layer version without skips is wildly non-convex. DenseNet, with its many skip connections, looked especially smooth.

The payoff:

higher usable learning rates
less sensitivity to initialization
faster convergence
minima in wider basins
6. Fewer degenerate plateaus
Orhan & Pitkow (2018) showed that skips remove three kinds of degeneracy that create flat regions and plateaus in plain networks:

Elimination: when a unit’s incoming or outgoing weights collapse to zero, as with a dead ReLU, the unit is effectively deleted and its other weights stop learning. With a skip, every unit still receives input through the identity and still reaches later layers.
Overlap: hidden units are interchangeable, so units with identical weights create degenerate directions. An identity skip breaks this symmetry, since unit i is tied to input channel i.
Linear dependence: units become linear combinations of one another, which skips make less likely.
7. Shallow at initialization, deep by the end
A skip alone doesn’t guarantee healthy forward signals. In an unnormalized ResNet with standard initialization, Var(x_{l+1}) ≈ Var(x_l) + Var(F(x_l)) ≈ 2·Var(x_l). Activations double each block and explode.

What the residual form provides is a dial: the scale of the branch relative to the shortcut. With batch norm inside the branch, each branch outputs roughly unit variance, while the stream’s variance grows linearly with depth. So block l contributes only about 1/√l of the signal. The network starts close to the identity, which makes it effectively shallow and easy to train (De & Smith, 2020).

Many successful tricks set this dial deliberately:

Zero-initializing the last BN scale in each block (Goyal et al., 2017).
Fixup, which trains 10,000-layer ResNets without normalization.
SkipInit and ReZero, which put a learnable scalar on each branch starting at 0.
LayerScale in deep ViTs.
1/√L multipliers on each branch.
DeepNorm, which upweights the shortcut to train 1,000-layer transformers.
The network begins as an easy shallow model and grows its effective depth as branches learn, a built-in curriculum. A plain network has no equivalent knob: you can’t scale your way to the identity.

8. What theory can prove
Theory works in simplified settings, but it consistently points the same way:

Hardt & Ma (2017): linear residual networks have no spurious critical points near the identity; every critical point there is a global minimum.
Shamir (2018); Kawaguchi & Bengio (2019): for certain residual architectures, every local minimum is at least as good as the best linear predictor. Adding residual layers can’t create minima worse than the simpler model.
Du et al. (2019): with enough width, gradient descent provably reaches zero training loss. The width needed grows exponentially with depth for plain nets but only polynomially for ResNets.
Part 2: Representation and information flow
9. Nothing is forced through a bottleneck
In a plain net, everything the output knows about the input must survive every layer. ReLUs zero out roughly half their inputs, dead units block information outright, and anything lost can’t be recovered downstream. In a residual net the shortcut carries x forward untouched and branches only add, so information lost inside a branch isn’t lost to the network. If each branch is a contraction (Lipschitz constant below 1), a residual block is provably invertible, so nothing is lost at all (Behrmann et al., 2019).

Skips also prevent specific collapse failures:

Rank collapse in transformers: stacked self-attention without skips drives all tokens toward the same representation, with rank decaying doubly exponentially in depth (Dong et al., 2021). Skip connections are the main thing that stops it.
Over-smoothing in graph networks: repeated neighbor averaging makes all node features identical. Residual connections, “initial residual” connections (GCNII) and jumping-knowledge skips are what make deep GNNs work.
Binary networks: Bi-Real Net adds an identity shortcut around every binarized convolution. This carries real-valued information past the 1-bit bottleneck and gives large accuracy gains.
10. Learning a residual is easier than learning a mapping
Deep in a network, each layer usually only nudges the representation, so the ideal transform H is close to the identity. Then F = H − x is small and a simpler target than H itself. He et al. confirmed that ResNet layer responses are smaller than those of plain nets, and smaller still in deeper ResNets.

This is reparameterization acting as preconditioning. He et al. drew explicitly on multigrid and hierarchical-basis preconditioning from numerical solvers, and on residual encodings like VLAD. Earlier, Raiko, Valpola & LeCun (2012) let a linear shortcut carry the linear part of a mapping, so nonlinear units only model what’s left. They showed this makes the problem better conditioned and speeds up SGD.

The “learn the delta” principle also works at the output:

VDSR super-resolution predicts only the high-frequency difference from an upsampled image.
DnCNN predicts just the noise.
Model-based RL predicts state changes rather than next states.
LoRA learns a weight update that starts at zero.
In each case the residual target is smaller, sparser and statistically simpler.

11. Iterative refinement
Because blocks add to a shared representation, consecutive blocks work in the same feature space, and x ← x + F(x) has the form of an update step. Greff et al. (2017) showed that within a stage, highway and residual nets refine the same features rather than computing new ones. They called this unrolled iterative estimation.

Jastrzębski et al. (2018) found that residual updates tend to align with the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the representation. In effect, blocks run something like gradient descent in feature space: early blocks make large changes, later blocks fine adjustments.

This explains why later blocks can often be dropped or shared cheaply. It also explains why the “logit lens” works on transformers: decoding the stream at intermediate layers gives predictions that sharpen layer by layer. And it fits the boosting analogy behind the name, since each block fits what’s left over.

12. The ODE view: smooth, stable transformations
x_{t+1} = x_t + h·f(x_t) is a forward-Euler step, so a deep ResNet approximates a continuous flow dx/dt = f(x, t) (E, 2017; Haber & Ruthotto, 2017; Lu et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018). That view brings several advantages:

Data is transformed by gradually deforming space, using continuous, nearly invertible maps that keep neighbors near each other.
ODE stability theory becomes a design tool. For example, constraining f’s Jacobian keeps propagation bounded and robust to perturbations.
Numerical analysis supplies architectures. Lu et al. showed PolyNet, FractalNet and RevNet match known integration schemes, and momentum ResNets and Neural ODEs followed.
Deeper networks become refinements of shallower ones, approaching a well-defined limit. That limit is what lets hyperparameters transfer across depth.
A plain net, x_{t+1} = f(x_t), has no such limit: every layer can send the state anywhere.

13. Feature reuse and multi-scale fusion
Dense connections. In DenseNet, each layer receives the concatenation of all earlier feature maps and adds only a few new channels. That has several effects:

Early features like edge detectors stay available to every later layer instead of being re-learned or carried along.
DenseNets matched ResNet accuracy with a fraction of the parameters.
Every layer sits a short hop from the loss, giving implicit deep supervision.
Concatenation keeps old and new features separate, where addition would mix them.
Encoder–decoder skips appear in U-Net, FCN, FPN and the U-Nets inside diffusion models. Downsampling builds semantics (“what”) but destroys spatial precision (“where”). Long skips carry high-resolution features straight to the decoder at the matching resolution, so the decoder only has to combine them. Without them, fine detail must squeeze through the bottleneck and be reconstructed, giving blurry, poorly localized outputs. FPN uses the same idea to give detectors strong semantics at every scale. This is about routing information, not just gradients.

14. The residual stream in transformers
Each sublayer reads from a shared stream and writes back additively:

x ← x + Attention(LN(x))
x ← x + MLP(LN(x))
That one structure does several jobs:

Trainability: Pre-LN keeps a clean, unnormalized path from embeddings to logits, so gradients behave well at initialization. Post-LN puts normalization on the main path, needs careful learning-rate warmup and is less stable at depth (Xiong et al., 2020).
A communication bus: any component can write into a subspace that a component many layers later reads (Elhage et al., 2021). This enables composition across layers. Induction heads, for instance, form when one head reads what an earlier “previous-token” head wrote.
Decomposability: because everything is a sum, the output splits into contributions from separate paths. These include the direct path from embedding to output (roughly bigram statistics), single-head paths, and compositions of heads. Tools like the logit lens, direct logit attribution and activation patching all rely on this.
Token identity: attention mixes information across positions, and the skip ensures each position keeps its own content.
Part 3: Generalization and robustness
15. Implicit ensembles of shallower networks
Each residual block can be taken or skipped, so n blocks create 2^n paths, most of length near n/2. Veit et al. (2016) found three telling things:

Deleting one block from a trained ResNet at test time barely changes accuracy. The exception is downsampling blocks, which lack a pure identity shortcut. Deleting a single layer from VGG drops it to chance.
Removing more blocks degrades accuracy smoothly, like removing members from an ensemble.
In a 110-layer ResNet with 54 blocks, almost all the training gradient flows through paths only about 5–17 blocks long.
So ResNets partly sidestep vanishing gradients by mainly training moderately short paths, and they gain ensemble-like robustness. Paths share weights and aren’t independent, so “ensemble” is an approximation. The flip side is that effective depth is lower than nominal depth.

16. Regularizers that only work with skips
Stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016) randomly drops whole residual branches during training, with survival probability decaying linearly to about 0.5 at the last block. The network still works because the identity carries the signal. The effects:

Training gets faster.
The model behaves like an ensemble of exponentially many sub-networks.
Test error drops; it even made 1,200-layer ResNets worthwhile.
Under the name DropPath it’s now standard in ViTs, Swin and ConvNeXt. LayerDrop does the same for transformers and also lets you prune layers at inference. In a plain net, dropping a layer severs the network.

17. Better-behaved function classes
A soft prior toward identity plus small corrections. With weight decay, this favors solutions that change the representation only where needed.
Kernels that don’t degenerate. In the neural-tangent-kernel regime, a deep plain net’s kernel loses its ability to tell inputs apart as depth grows. A ResNet’s kernel, with suitably scaled branches, doesn’t (Huang et al., 2020). This is the generalization side of the input collapse from #3.
Flatter minima, per Li et al.’s visualizations, often (though debatably) linked to better generalization.
Skips aren’t mainly a regularizer, though. ResNet-1202 reached near-zero training error on CIFAR-10 but tested worse than ResNet-110. Once optimization is solved, overfitting becomes the limit.

Part 4: Engineering benefits
18. Networks you can grow, shrink and adapt
A block whose branch outputs zero is exactly the identity, which makes networks editable:

Grow: insert new blocks with zero-initialized output layers without changing the function, as in Net2Net-style growth and LLaMA Pro’s block expansion.
Adapt: adapters are inserted residually and start near zero, so fine-tuning begins from the pretrained behavior. LoRA initializes its B matrix to zero for the same reason. ControlNet’s “zero convolutions” attach a conditioning network to a frozen diffusion model without disturbing it.
Shrink or skip: layer pruning, early exits, dynamic depth (SkipNet, BlockDrop) and Mixture-of-Depths all work because skipping a block leaves a valid network. In Mixture-of-Depths, tokens route around blocks along the residual path.
19. Reversibility and memory savings
With additive coupling, y1 = x1 + F(x2) and y2 = x2 + G(y1) invert exactly: x2 = y2 − G(y1), then x1 = y1 − F(x2). RevNets and the Reformer use this to recompute activations during backprop instead of storing them, which makes activation memory nearly independent of depth. Invertible ResNets and Residual Flows use the same property for normalizing flows with exact likelihoods.

20. Cheap, robust and tuning-friendly
An identity shortcut has no parameters and costs one addition. He et al. compared plain and residual nets at identical size and compute.
ResNets converge faster early on even when final accuracy matches, as ResNet-18 did against plain-18.
They tolerate a wider range of initializations, learning rates and activations. The zero-init trick from #7 was one ingredient of fast large-batch ImageNet training.
With 1/√L branch scaling, good hyperparameters transfer across depth (Yang et al., 2023; Bordelon et al., 2023). You can tune a shallow model and scale it up.
21. The same trick through time
In the original 1997 LSTM, the memory cell’s self-connection had a fixed weight of 1. That is literally an identity skip across time steps: the “constant error carousel” that beat vanishing gradients in RNNs. The forget gate added later made it a gated, highway-style skip, and the GRU’s (1 − z)·h + z·h̃ is the same idea.

Highway networks were explicitly LSTM’s trick turned sideways into depth, and ResNets are roughly highway networks with the gates fixed open. Residual links between stacked LSTM layers made Google’s 8-layer translation system (GNMT) trainable, and WaveNet is built on residual and skip connections. Shortcut connections in MLPs go back to at least the late 1980s.

Part 5: What’s really doing the work
The weight of evidence says the main effect is on optimization, not on what a network can represent:

The degradation problem showed up in training error.
RepVGG trains each layer as a 3×3 conv plus a 1×1 conv plus, where shapes match, an identity branch. It then merges them algebraically into one 3×3 conv, giving a plain VGG-style network that computes the identical function. Training that plain architecture directly gives noticeably worse accuracy. Same function class, different trainability.
Deep plain nets can be trained without skips. Delta-orthogonal initialization trained 10,000-layer vanilla CNNs (Xiao et al., 2018), and kernel-shaping methods like Deep Kernel Shaping and TAT have closed much of the gap to ResNets. But it takes heavy machinery and stays fragile, while skips get there for the cost of an addition.
The other benefits are real too: information preservation, feature reuse, multi-scale fusion, the transformer communication bus, implicit ensembles and modularity. In U-Nets, DenseNets and transformers they’re arguably as important as trainability.

Costs and caveats:

Memory and bandwidth: long skips and dense concatenations keep activations alive longer.
Latency: graphs with parallel branches are less hardware-friendly, which is what motivated RepVGG.
Variance growth: without normalization or careful branch scaling, residual activations grow with depth.
Diminishing depth: in Pre-LN transformers, the residual stream’s norm grows with depth, so later layers’ writes count for relatively less. Many deep LLM layers can be pruned with little loss, a problem sometimes called the “curse of depth.”
Overfitting: it returns once optimization is no longer the bottleneck.
