2. They mitigate vanishing and exploding gradients
The identity path is a “gradient highway.” In a plain network, gradients must pass through every nonlinearity and weight matrix. In a residual network, gradients can bypass layers entirely. This reduces the chance that repeated multiplication by small matrices kills the gradient, or that repeated multiplication by large matrices amplifies it. The same idea underlies temporal skip connections in recurrent networks and gated shortcuts in LSTMs/GRUs: direct paths through time reduce vanishing gradients over long sequences.

4. They address the degradation problem
Very deep plain networks often show higher training error than shallower ones—not because they overfit, but because they are harder to optimize. This is the degradation problem. Skip connections allow depth to increase without making optimization catastrophically harder. The network can always fall back to a shorter, near-identity path, and additional layers can learn incremental refinements. This is why residual networks can be hundreds of layers deep while plain networks of similar depth often fail to train well.

6. They smooth or simplify the loss landscape
Skip connections tend to produce loss landscapes that are easier to optimize: fewer pathological singularities, less extreme curvature, and better-behaved gradients. The identity path prevents the network from becoming a long chain of transformations whose composition can create very sharp or chaotic behavior. This does not mean the landscape becomes convex; it means the optimization geometry is often friendlier. This is one reason residual connections are used in very deep transformers, GANs, diffusion models, and other hard-to-train systems.

7. They reduce the effective depth needed for simple examples
A residual network can be viewed as having many paths of different lengths. For an easy input, the network can effectively use a short path; for a hard input, it can use deeper paths. This gives an adaptive-depth or “shortcut” interpretation: not every example must pass through every nonlinear transformation. The network is not literally forced to choose a single path, but the presence of short paths changes the inductive bias and makes simple functions easier to represent and optimize.

8. They preserve information in the forward pass
In a plain network, information must pass through every layer, and each nonlinearity can distort or discard it. A skip connection carries the original activation forward directly. This preserves identity information, low-level features, and fine details. In autoencoders, this helps avoid losing input detail through the bottleneck. In segmentation networks, it helps recover spatial detail. In transformers, the residual stream preserves token identity across many layers.

10. They support multi-scale representations
In U-Net-style architectures, skip connections combine deep, semantic, low-resolution features with shallow, high-resolution, spatially precise features. 12. They reduce co-adaptation and act as a regularizer
Because information and gradients can bypass layers, individual layers are less forced to co-adapt perfectly with all others. Stochastic depth and dropout-like skipping of blocks exploit this: training randomly sampled sub-networks prevents over-reliance on particular layers. Dense connections can also reduce redundancy by encouraging reuse. This regularization effect is not the only reason skips help, but it is part of why they often improve generalization rather than just training speed.

13. They make networks modular and incrementally refinable
With residual connections, each block can learn a small additive correction. This gives a “boosting-like” or iterative-refinement interpretation: the representation is updated step by step, and earlier information is preserved. It also makes architecture design more modular: you can add blocks without forcing the network to relearn the identity, and you can prune or drop blocks with less catastrophic damage. Layers can effectively be ignored if they are not useful.

15. They alleviate oversmoothing in graph neural networks
In deep graph neural networks, repeated message passing can make node representations converge toward similar values—oversmoothing. This is the graph-network analogue of preserving information and gradient flow in deep feedforward networks.

16. They help recurrent networks remember over time
Temporal skip connections in recurrent networks create direct paths across time steps. This reduces vanishing gradients through long sequences and helps the model retain information over long horizons. Gated recurrent architectures such as LSTMs and GRUs can be interpreted as learning adaptive skip connections: gates decide how much of the previous state to carry forward and how much to update.

19. They improve training stability in generative and adversarial models
In GANs, diffusion models, and image-to-image systems, skip connections stabilize training and preserve fine detail. U-Net-style skips let the generator or denoiser combine global semantic information with local high-frequency information. Residual connections in the discriminator or generator improve gradient flow. This is one reason modern diffusion architectures are heavily residual and U-Net-like.

20. They can improve generalization, not just trainability
Skip connections often improve test performance, but the reason is not simply “more capacity.” They change the inductive bias toward identity-preserving, incrementally refined, multi-path, multi-scale representations. Implicit ensembling, feature reuse, regularization from stochastic depth, and better optimization can all contribute. However, this is not guaranteed: skip connections can also make it easier to propagate noise or bypass useful nonlinear processing, and concatenation skips can add parameters and memory cost.

21. They complement normalization and initialization
Skip connections are often paired with normalization and careful initialization. The identity path keeps signal and gradient scales reasonable, while normalization controls activation statistics. Neither alone is a complete explanation: residual connections can cause variance growth if unnormalized, and normalization alone does not solve deep optimization. Together they make very deep networks practical.

Bottom line
Skip connections improve neural networks because they change several things at once:

Gradients: they provide identity/short paths, turning long products into sums over paths and reducing vanishing/exploding gradients.

Optimization: they make identity mappings easy, improve conditioning, smooth the loss landscape, and address the degradation problem.

Information flow: they preserve activations, low-level features, spatial detail, and token identity.

Representation: they enable feature reuse, multi-scale fusion, incremental refinement, and modularity.

Ensembling and regularization: they create many paths, support stochastic depth, reduce co-adaptation, and can improve robustness/generalization.

Stability: they stabilize deep transformers, GNNs, RNNs, GANs, diffusion models, and dynamical-system-style architectures.

Architecture-specific benefits: U-Net detail recovery, DenseNet reuse, Transformer residual streams, GNN oversmoothing mitigation, and recurrent temporal shortcuts.

The deepest single explanation is the Jacobian one: skip connections ensure that the network and its gradient are never forced through only long multiplicative chains. Everything else—identity learning, feature reuse, multi-path ensembles, multi-scale fusion, and stability—builds on that basic change in information and gradient geometry.
